## Description

The EU Cyber Resilience Act is the first EU-wide law to put binding cybersecurity requirements on software and hardware products across every sector, and the first to give open source its own legal role: the open source steward. From 11 December 2027 it decides which products may be placed on the EU market at all, and its reach goes well beyond Europe, because any vendor selling there must account for every component in its product, wherever that code was written.

For most of its history, security in open source has run on goodwill. That is changing. As governments start writing security into law, a security policy, a way to report vulnerabilities and timely fixes stop being good practice and become something regulators, customers and downstream vendors expect to see. This matters to everyone in the ecosystem: users deciding which projects they can depend on, contributors, project owners, and the enterprises behind open source projects.

In this session we will explain what the CRA actually is and the positions an open source project can be in: out of scope, open source steward, or manufacturer, with a simple test to tell them apart. We will cover what an open source project is expected to do to comply, what vendors building on open source will be required to do, and what they will start asking project maintainers for. We will also talk about where open source security is heading as laws like the CRA come into force. We maintain open source projects ourselves and at work, we help companies comply with standards such as ISO 27001, the CRA, GDPR and NIS2, and from that, we will share a practical checklist drawn from applying all 71 articles of the regulation to real projects.

This session will be a complete map of what the Cyber Resilience Act means for open source, and of where open source security is headed as a result. Attendees will leave with a clear understanding of what the CRA is, which role their project falls into, exactly what that role requires and by when, and a concrete checklist they can start working through the next morning.

## Benefits to the ecosystem

Open source is at a turning point. For years its security has been a matter of best practice. The Cyber Resilience Act, which entered into force in December 2024 and applies in full from 11 December 2027, turns that into a legal matter, with a named role for the organisations behind open source, dated obligations, and real consequences for the vendors who ship it. The security side of open source is clearly moving from following best practices to complying with the law, and the CRA is only the first of these laws.

Most of the engineers who actually maintain this code have not heard that this is happening. We believe the whole open source world, from maintainers and contributors to the enterprises that fund projects or sell products built on them, needs to know what the CRA is and what it requires of them if their software reaches the EU market.

Our aim with this talk is to give the community that knowledge in one session: which role a project falls into, the exact duties and dates for each role, what downstream vendors will now ask of maintainers, and a short checklist any project can act on. This session also shows how the new duties on vendors can work in the ecosystem's favour, turning vulnerability handling into a shared responsibility between maintainers and the companies that depend on them, instead of a burden carried by volunteers. Everything in it comes from applying the regulation to open source projects we maintain ourselves.
